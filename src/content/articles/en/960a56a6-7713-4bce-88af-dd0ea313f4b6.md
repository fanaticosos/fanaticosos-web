**Chicago won in Atlanta, improved its record to 4-2 and recovered two key starters. But behind the 31-23 score lies more interesting stories than the scoreboard: a precision record by Caleb Williams, a defensive gamble that Dennis Allen had imagined for over a year, and a banana-style punt that helped change the game.**

Fans who watched the game remember Caleb's return, the 31-7 lead and the scare in the fourth quarter. What deserves a second look is *how* Chicago built that superiority and why it was so close to complicating a victory that seemed decided.

## Caleb Williams: 24 of 24 when the pocket was clean

Caleb returned after missing three games due to a hamstring injury and completed **30 of 36 passes for 305 yards, a touchdown and no interceptions**. His **83.3 % efficiency** was a Bears record among quarterbacks with at least 30 attempts in a game.

But the number that really stands out is another: **24 completions in 24 attempts from a clean pocket, for 248 yards**. According to Next Gen Stats, no one had accumulated that many attempts without failing under those conditions in a game since the record began, at least since 2018.

The distinction matters. It doesn't mean Williams was perfect on every throw, but that, when the line gave him time, **he didn't waste a single opportunity recorded in that category**. He also opened the game with 12 consecutive completions, his best streak personally.

The offensive coordinator Press Taylor acknowledged that the main concern was the lack of rhythm after nearly a month without competing. Monday he explained that Williams worked additional throws Wednesday to stay sharp, even before a light team practice. The preparation was evident: he connected with eight different teammates, including Luther Burden III and Colston Loveland.

And there's a stat to remember: with his touchdown pass, Williams reached **50 rushing touchdowns before throwing 15 interceptions**, a combination that, according to the stats released by the Bears, only Patrick Mahomes and Lamar Jackson had achieved in NFL history.

## Kyler Gordon: Dennis Allen's idea finally came to life

In January 2025, when Dennis Allen arrived in Chicago, he specifically mentioned Gordon when talking about the secondary. It wasn't a coincidence. He saw in the slot cornerback a player capable of doing more than covering receivers.

Injuries delayed the experiment. Until Atlanta.

In just **17 defensive plays**, Gordon produced **three sacks and two forced fumbles**, all the sacks in the second half. Since sacks are an official statistic, since 1982, only **two defensive secondary players** have achieved at least three sacks and two forced fumbles in a single game.

Allen explained Monday that Gordon has a rare combination of speed, change of direction and instinct for finding the quarterback. It's not simply sending a cornerback on a blitz: it's choosing one who can evade blocks and cut across the field before the passer adjusts his protection.

The demonstration came when it mattered most. Atlanta had cut the score to **31-23** and still had a chance to tie. With **2:54** remaining, Gordon attacked Michael Penix Jr., stripped the ball and **Devin Bush recovered the fumble in the Falcons' 28-yard line**.

**Chicago built the lead with its offense. Gordon prevented it from being wasted.**

## The banana-style punt that no one saw coming

The special teams also left a story worth repeating. The coordinator Richard Hightower explained that Tory Taylor used a lateral punt, known as *banana ball*, a technique he developed from his years playing Australian football.

The ball travels with an uncomfortable spin for the returner. In Atlanta, that difficulty ended in a poorly received punt that **Josh Blackwell recovered** for Chicago. Hightower highlighted that Blackwell reached approximately **21 miles per hour** in coverage.

It wasn't the only fumble of that unit: **Zavier Scott forced a fumble on a kickoff return and Nick McCloud recovered it**. It was the first time since October 2018 that the Bears' special teams achieved two recoveries in a single game.

Plays that rarely make the headlines, but help explain why Chicago was able to establish the rhythm and build such a large lead.

## The red zone changed its face; the problem wasn't the playbook

Before traveling to Atlanta, Chicago had scored touchdowns in just **9 of 21 visits to the red zone: 42.9 %**, the worst efficiency in the NFL at that time.

Against the Falcons, the team converted **four of five opportunities** inside the 20-yard line into touchdowns. D'Andre Swift scored twice on the ground and Roschon Johnson added another rushing touchdown. Loveland caught the touchdown pass.

What's notable is the explanation from Press Taylor: **there was no change in the scheme**. According to the coordinator, the opportunities were already there; this time the players executed better. It's an important difference for evaluating Ben Johnson: not every problem requires a new play in the playbook, sometimes it requires finishing the one already designed correctly.

The offense held the ball for **40:08**, and that possession was also defensive against Bijan Robinson. The Atlanta rusher came into the game as the league's leading rusher with 580 yards; he finished with **61 yards on nine carries**. Dennis Allen acknowledged that the early lead limited the Falcons' opportunities to feed him. It wasn't just a matter of stopping him: Chicago helped take him out of the game script.

## The uncomfortable stat: 12 penalties and 133 yards gifted

All of the above explains why Chicago was up **31-7**. But it also makes it harder to justify the outcome.

The Bears committed **12 penalties for 133 yards**. Atlanta found life in the fourth quarter, Michael Penix Jr. connected two touchdowns with Kyle Pitts and two two-point conversions narrowed the gap to eight.

Gordon resolved the final threat, yes. But relying on an extraordinary individual play to protect a 24-point lead cannot become a closing plan. That's the contradiction Chicago will have to face before hosting New England on Thursday, October 22.

## The columnist's note | A lot of talent, too many gifts

Atlanta's victory allows two interpretations and it would be a mistake to choose only the most comfortable one.

The first is encouraging: Caleb Williams returned with an unprecedented precision record for the franchise, Kyler Gordon turned a longtime idea of Dennis Allen into a historic performance, and the special teams produced two recovered balls. There is talent, preparation and real tactical resources.

The second is uncomfortable: **a team that dominates the ball for more than 40 minutes and comes out on top by 24 points shouldn't end up waiting for a strip-sack savior**. Twelve penalties for 133 yards aren't a footnote. They're opportunities that are gifted, series that are extended, and an invitation for the opponent to come back into the game.

Chicago leaves Atlanta with a **4-2** record, but the real test isn't repeating Gordon's three sacks. That would be asking for another miracle. It's about playing with enough discipline so that a large lead doesn't need one.

And now comes New England in a short week. **Are we seeing Bears capable of competing with anyone, or a team still trying to complicate things on its own?**
